Mastering Medicare: Essential Tips to Avoid Common Enrollment Mistakes

Published May 18, 2025

Medicare enrollment sounds straightforward — you turn 65, you sign up, you're covered. But we see the same mistakes over and over, and they cost people money, coverage gaps, and sometimes lifelong penalties.

The good news? Every one of these mistakes is avoidable if you know what to look for.

Here are the seven most common Medicare enrollment mistakes we see — and how to avoid them.

Mistake #1: Missing the Initial Enrollment Period

Your Initial Enrollment Period (IEP) is a 7-month window around your 65th birthday:

  • 3 months before your birthday month
  • Your birthday month
  • 3 months after your birthday month

If you miss this window and don't have creditable coverage (like employer insurance), you'll have to wait until the General Enrollment Period (January 1–March 31) to sign up. That means a gap in coverage. And it gets worse: you'll also pay a late enrollment penalty for Part B that lasts for life.

The Part B penalty is 10% of the standard premium for every 12-month period you were eligible but didn't enroll. So if you delay 2 years, you'll pay 20% more — forever.

How to avoid it: Mark your calendar 3 months before your 65th birthday. If you're still working with employer coverage, verify whether you need to enroll now or can delay without penalty (see Mistake #2).

Use our enrollment calculator to see your exact IEP dates.

Mistake #2: Assuming You Need to Sign Up at 65 If You Have Employer Coverage

If you or your spouse are still working and have employer group health insurance that's considered creditable coverage, you may be able to delay Medicare Part B without penalty.

But the rules are specific:

  • The employer must have 20 or more employees.
  • The coverage must be active group coverage (not retiree coverage, not COBRA).
  • You must enroll in Medicare Part B within 8 months of leaving that job or losing that coverage.

If the employer has fewer than 20 employees, Medicare becomes primary and you should enroll at 65 to avoid gaps and penalties.

How to avoid it: Don't assume. Talk to your HR department or call us. We help people navigate this decision every week.

Mistake #3: Not Enrolling in Part D Because "I Don't Take Medications"

Even if you take zero prescriptions today, you should still enroll in a Part D plan during your Initial Enrollment Period.

Why? Because if you go 63 days or more without creditable drug coverage after your IEP ends, you'll pay a late enrollment penalty when you eventually sign up. That penalty is 1% of the national base premium for every month you went without coverage — and it lasts for life.

The good news: there are $0 premium Part D plans in many areas. You can enroll in one of these plans as a placeholder, and if you never use it, you're still protected from the penalty.

How to avoid it: Enroll in a Part D plan (even a $0 premium plan) during your IEP. You can always switch plans during the Annual Enrollment Period (October 15–December 7) if your medication needs change.

Mistake #4: Picking a Plan Based on Premium Alone

A $0 premium Medicare Advantage plan sounds great — until you see a specialist and pay a $60 copay every visit. Or until you fill a prescription and find out it's $400/month because it's on Tier 4.

We see this constantly: people choose the lowest-premium plan without looking at copays, prescription costs, or the annual out-of-pocket maximum.

A $0 premium plan can cost you more than a $50/month plan if you use healthcare regularly.

How to avoid it: Look at the total cost picture:

  • Monthly premium
  • Copays for primary care and specialist visits
  • Copays for prescriptions (check your medications, not generic examples)
  • Copays for hospital stays, outpatient surgery, imaging
  • Annual maximum out-of-pocket limit

Mistake #5: Not Verifying Doctors Are In-Network Before Enrolling

This is the most common complaint we hear: "I enrolled in this plan and then found out my doctor isn't covered."

Every Medicare Advantage plan has a network. If your primary care doctor or specialists aren't in that network, you'll either pay out-of-pocket (if it's a PPO) or you won't be covered at all (if it's an HMO).

Don't trust the online provider directories — they're often outdated. Call your doctor's office directly and ask: "Do you accept [carrier name] Medicare Advantage?"

How to avoid it: Before you enroll, verify your doctors are in-network. Or ask us — we check this for every client before enrollment.

Mistake #6: Missing the SEP After Losing Employer Coverage

When your employer coverage ends (retirement, layoff, company closes), you get a Special Enrollment Period (SEP) to enroll in Medicare Part B without penalty.

That SEP lasts 8 months from the date your coverage ends (or the date you stop working, whichever is later).

If you miss that 8-month window, you'll have to wait until the General Enrollment Period (January 1–March 31) and you'll pay a late enrollment penalty.

How to avoid it: As soon as you know your employer coverage is ending, start the Medicare enrollment process. Don't wait until the last minute. We help people navigate this transition regularly — call us as soon as you get notice.

Mistake #7: Not Reviewing Your Plan Annually During AEP

Medicare Advantage and Part D plans change every year. Your doctor might leave the network. Your prescription might move to a higher tier. A better plan might launch in your area.

The Annual Enrollment Period (October 15–December 7) is your chance to switch plans if your current one no longer fits.

We see people stay on the same plan for years without reviewing it — and end up paying hundreds more than they need to.

How to avoid it: Review your plan every year during AEP. Check:

  • Are your doctors still in-network?
  • Are your prescriptions still covered at the same cost tier?
  • Did the premium, copays, or out-of-pocket maximum change?
  • Are there new plans in your area with better coverage or lower costs?

A 20-minute annual review can save you hundreds or even thousands of dollars.

FAQs

What happens if I miss my Initial Enrollment Period?

You'll have to wait until the General Enrollment Period (January 1–March 31) to enroll. Your coverage will start July 1, leaving you with a gap. You'll also pay a late enrollment penalty for Part B — 10% of the standard premium for every 12-month period you delayed.

Can I delay Medicare if I have employer insurance?

Yes, if your employer has 20 or more employees and you have active group coverage (not COBRA, not retiree coverage). When that coverage ends, you have 8 months to enroll in Part B without penalty.

Do I really need Part D if I don't take any medications?

Yes. If you go 63 days or more without creditable drug coverage after your Initial Enrollment Period ends, you'll pay a lifelong late enrollment penalty when you eventually sign up. Enroll in a $0 premium Part D plan as a placeholder.

Can I switch Medicare plans if I made a mistake?

Yes. You can switch Medicare Advantage or Part D plans during the Annual Enrollment Period (October 15–December 7) or the Medicare Advantage Open Enrollment Period (January 1–March 31). If you want to switch from Medicare Advantage back to Traditional Medicare and buy a Medigap plan, you may face medical underwriting unless you're within your Medigap Open Enrollment Period.
