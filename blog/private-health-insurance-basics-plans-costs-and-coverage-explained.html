Private Health Insurance Basics: Plans, Costs, and Coverage Explained

Published January 14, 2026

If you've ever stared at an open enrollment packet and felt completely lost, you're not alone. Private health insurance comes with its own language — premiums, deductibles, copays, networks — and it's easy to feel like you need a translator just to pick a plan. This guide breaks it all down in plain English so you can make a confident decision for yourself or your family.

What Is Private Health Insurance?

Private health insurance is coverage you get outside of government programs like Medicare or Medicaid. It can come from your employer, be purchased directly from an insurance company, or obtained through the ACA Marketplace (Healthcare.gov). The insurer agrees to cover a portion of your medical costs — hospital stays, doctor visits, prescriptions, preventive care — in exchange for a monthly premium.

Unlike Medicare, which is federally standardized, private insurance plans vary widely in what they cover, how much they cost, and which doctors and hospitals are included in the network. That's why comparing plans before you enroll matters so much.

The Main Types of Private Health Insurance Plans

HMO — Health Maintenance Organization

HMOs require you to choose a primary care physician (PCP) who coordinates all your care. You'll need a referral from your PCP to see a specialist, and coverage is generally limited to in-network providers. HMOs tend to have lower premiums and out-of-pocket costs, but less flexibility in who you can see.

Best for: People who want predictable costs and don't mind staying within a defined network.

PPO — Preferred Provider Organization

PPOs give you more freedom. You can see any doctor or specialist — in or out of network — without a referral. Out-of-network care is covered, just at a higher cost. PPOs typically carry higher premiums but are popular with people who have established relationships with specific doctors or specialists.

Best for: People who travel frequently, have ongoing specialist care, or want maximum flexibility.

EPO — Exclusive Provider Organization

An EPO is a hybrid. Like a PPO, you don't need referrals. Like an HMO, you're restricted to an in-network provider list — except in emergencies. Go out of network and you pay 100% of the bill. EPOs often have lower premiums than PPOs while keeping the no-referral convenience.

Best for: People who want no-referral access but are comfortable staying in-network.

HDHP — High-Deductible Health Plan

HDHPs pair a lower monthly premium with a higher deductible — meaning you pay more out of pocket before insurance kicks in. They're often paired with a Health Savings Account (HSA), which lets you set aside pre-tax dollars for medical expenses. If you're relatively healthy and rarely use insurance, an HDHP can save you money over the year.

Best for: Younger, healthier individuals or those who want to build an HSA for future medical costs.

Key Terms You Need to Know

Understanding these terms will help you compare plans accurately — not just by premium, but by total potential cost.

  • Premium: The monthly amount you pay to keep the plan active, regardless of whether you use it.
  • Deductible: The amount you pay out of pocket each year before your insurance starts sharing costs. A $3,000 deductible means you cover the first $3,000 in medical bills.
  • Copay: A fixed amount you pay per visit or prescription (e.g., $30 to see your doctor). Copays usually apply before or independently of the deductible.
  • Coinsurance: After you meet your deductible, you split remaining costs with the insurer at a set percentage — for example, 80/20 means the plan pays 80%, you pay 20%.
  • Out-of-Pocket Maximum: The most you'll ever pay in a plan year. Once you hit this limit, insurance covers 100% of covered services for the rest of the year. This is your financial safety net.
  • Network: The group of doctors, hospitals, and facilities that have contracted with your insurer. In-network care costs less; out-of-network care may cost more or not be covered at all.
  • Prior Authorization: Some procedures or medications require advance approval from your insurer before they're covered.

Where Can You Get Private Health Insurance?

Through Your Employer

Employer-sponsored insurance is the most common type. Your employer typically pays a portion of the premium, making it one of the most affordable options. During open enrollment (usually in the fall), you choose from the plans your employer offers. Outside of that window, you generally can't make changes unless you have a qualifying life event.

The ACA Marketplace (Healthcare.gov)

If you don't have access to employer coverage, the ACA Marketplace is the main place to shop for private insurance. Plans are organized into metal tiers — Bronze, Silver, Gold, and Platinum — ranging from lower-premium/higher-deductible to higher-premium/lower-deductible. Depending on your income, you may qualify for premium tax credits that significantly reduce your monthly cost.

Open enrollment for Marketplace plans typically runs from November 1 through January 15 in Florida. Outside of open enrollment, you need a Special Enrollment Period (SEP) triggered by a qualifying event.

Directly from an Insurance Company or Broker

You can purchase plans directly from insurers like Aetna, Cigna, BlueCross BlueShield, or Oscar — or through a licensed broker who can compare options across multiple companies at no extra cost to you. Working with a broker is often the smartest move because they can match your specific situation (doctors, medications, budget) to the right plan, and you pay the same price you would going direct.

What About COBRA?

If you lose employer-sponsored coverage — due to job loss, reduced hours, or leaving a job — COBRA lets you continue your existing employer plan for up to 18 months (sometimes longer). The catch: you now pay the full premium yourself, including the portion your employer used to cover. COBRA can be expensive, often $500–$700/month for an individual.

For many people, a Marketplace plan or a private plan through a broker ends up being more affordable than COBRA. You have 60 days from losing coverage to elect COBRA, but you also have 60 days to enroll in a Marketplace plan through a Special Enrollment Period — so it's worth comparing both before you decide.

What Does Private Insurance Cover?

Under the ACA, all qualifying health plans must cover 10 essential health benefits:

  1. Ambulatory (outpatient) care
  2. Emergency services
  3. Hospitalization
  4. Maternity and newborn care
  5. Mental health and substance use disorder services
  6. Prescription drugs
  7. Rehabilitative and habilitative services
  8. Laboratory services
  9. Preventive and wellness services
  10. Pediatric services, including dental and vision for children

Note that adult dental and vision are typically not included in standard health plans — those usually require separate coverage.

How to Choose the Right Plan

There's no single "best" plan — it depends on your situation. Here's a simple framework:

  • How often do you use healthcare? If you have regular prescriptions, specialist visits, or ongoing conditions, a higher-premium plan with lower cost-sharing often saves money in the long run.
  • Do you have preferred doctors? Check whether your current doctors are in-network before choosing a plan. Switching to a cheaper plan that doesn't include your cardiologist or OB-GYN can cost you more in the end.
  • What prescriptions do you take? Each plan has a formulary (drug list). Verify your medications are covered and at what tier — tier 3 or 4 drugs can be very expensive even with insurance.
  • What's your budget? Don't just look at the premium. Add up the premium plus a realistic estimate of what you'd pay out of pocket based on how much care you typically use.
  • Are you eligible for subsidies? If your income is between 100% and 400% of the federal poverty level, you likely qualify for ACA premium tax credits that can dramatically reduce your costs.
