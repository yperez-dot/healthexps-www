Understanding How Income Affects Medicare Premiums: A Complete Guide for Retirees

Published July 6, 2025

Most people know Medicare isn't entirely free. What surprises them is finding out they'll pay more than their neighbor — sometimes a lot more — based on income from two years ago.

⚠️ Married filing separately? Your IRMAA works differently.

If you lived with your spouse at any point during the year but filed separately, there's no gradual bracket — income above $109,000 puts you at $649.20/month, and above $391,000 you're at the top tier, $689.90/month. There's no room in between.

Source: SSA POMS HI 01101.020

That surcharge is called IRMAA, and if you're a higher earner (or had a one-time spike in income), it can add hundreds of dollars per month to your Medicare costs.

Here's how it works, what triggers it, and what you can do about it.

Medicare Premiums: The Basics

Medicare is divided into four parts:

  • Part A (Hospital): Free for most people if you or your spouse worked and paid Medicare taxes for at least 10 years.
  • Part B (Medical): Covers doctor visits, outpatient care, preventive services. Standard premium in 2026 is $202.90/month.
  • Part C (Medicare Advantage): Private plans that replace Parts A + B. Premiums vary by plan.
  • Part D (Prescription Drugs): Standalone drug coverage or bundled into Medicare Advantage. Premiums vary by plan.

If your income is below certain thresholds, you pay the standard Part B premium ($202.90 in 2026). If your income is above those thresholds, you pay more.

What Is IRMAA?

IRMAA stands for Income-Related Monthly Adjustment Amount. It's a surcharge added to your Part B and Part D premiums if your income exceeds certain levels.

Here's the key detail most people miss: Medicare uses your income from two years ago.

For 2026, Medicare looks at your 2024 tax return. If your Modified Adjusted Gross Income (MAGI) in 2024 was above the thresholds, you'll pay IRMAA in 2026.

Who Pays IRMAA in 2026?

IRMAA kicks in when your 2024 MAGI exceeds:

  • Single filers: $109,000
  • Joint filers: $218,000

Above those thresholds, you move into IRMAA tiers. The higher your income, the higher your surcharge.

At the highest tier (single filers above $500,000 or joint filers above $750,000), you'll pay significantly more than the standard premium.

What Counts as Income for IRMAA?

IRMAA is based on your Modified Adjusted Gross Income (MAGI), which includes:

  • Wages and salaries
  • Social Security benefits (taxable portion)
  • Pensions and annuities
  • Investment income (dividends, capital gains, interest)
  • Rental income
  • IRA and 401(k) distributions
  • Roth conversions

Even if you're retired, you can still trigger IRMAA. A large IRA withdrawal, selling a home, or a Roth conversion can push you over the threshold.

Can You Appeal IRMAA?

Yes. If your income dropped significantly due to a life-changing event, you can appeal using Form SSA-44.

Life-changing events include:

  • Retirement (you or your spouse)
  • Death of a spouse
  • Divorce or annulment
  • Loss of income-producing property (due to disaster or similar event)
  • Employer settlement payment stopped

If approved, Social Security will use your current income instead of your income from two years ago. This can save you hundreds per month.

Form SSA-44 is called the "Medicare Income-Related Monthly Adjustment Amount — Life-Changing Event" form. You can file it online, by mail, or in person at a Social Security office.

Strategies to Minimize IRMAA

If you're approaching the IRMAA thresholds or already paying surcharges, here are strategies to consider:

1. Plan Retirement Income Withdrawals Carefully

Withdraw just enough from IRAs and 401(k)s to stay below the next IRMAA tier. Even $1 over the threshold moves you to the next bracket.

2. Consider Roth Conversions Early

Roth conversions count as income in the year you convert. If you're planning conversions, do them before you enroll in Medicare (or in low-income years) to avoid triggering IRMAA later.

3. Use Qualified Charitable Distributions (QCDs)

If you're 70½ or older, you can donate up to $105,000 per year directly from your IRA to charity. QCDs satisfy your Required Minimum Distribution (RMD) but don't count as income for IRMAA purposes.

4. Delay Social Security if Possible

Delaying Social Security reduces your current taxable income (and IRMAA exposure) while increasing your monthly benefit later.

5. Work with a Tax Professional

IRMAA planning intersects with tax planning, retirement income planning, and Medicare enrollment. A CPA or financial planner who understands Medicare can help you navigate the brackets.

Medicare Advantage vs. Medicare Supplement: Does Income Matter?

IRMAA applies to your Part B premium regardless of whether you choose Original Medicare or Medicare Advantage. You'll pay the surcharge either way.

However, your total Medicare costs do vary based on whether you choose Medicare Advantage or a Medicare Supplement (Medigap) plan.

  • Medicare Advantage: Typically lower premiums ($0 to $100/month), but higher out-of-pocket costs if you use a lot of healthcare.
  • Medicare Supplement: Higher premiums ($300–$375/month for Plan G in Florida), but predictable out-of-pocket costs and no network restrictions.

Medicare Enrollment Periods

Understanding IRMAA is important, but so is enrolling on time. Missing your enrollment window can trigger late enrollment penalties that last for life.

Key enrollment periods:

  • Initial Enrollment Period (IEP): 7 months around your 65th birthday (3 months before, month of, 3 months after).
  • General Enrollment Period (GEP): January 1–March 31 each year (if you missed IEP).
  • Annual Enrollment Period (AEP): October 15–December 7 each year (to switch Medicare Advantage or Part D plans).

FAQs

What is IRMAA?

IRMAA is an income-based surcharge added to your Medicare Part B and Part D premiums. It applies if your income exceeds $109,000 (single) or $218,000 (joint) based on your tax return from two years ago.

How does Social Security income affect Medicare premiums?

The taxable portion of your Social Security benefits counts toward your Modified Adjusted Gross Income (MAGI), which is used to determine IRMAA. Higher Social Security benefits can push you into a higher IRMAA bracket.

What is Form SSA-44?

Form SSA-44 is the form you file to appeal your IRMAA determination if you experienced a life-changing event (retirement, death of spouse, divorce, etc.) that reduced your income.

Can you appeal IRMAA?

Yes. If your income dropped due to a qualifying life-changing event, you can file Form SSA-44 to request Social Security use your current income instead of income from two years ago.
